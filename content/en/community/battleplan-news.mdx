## What Actually Makes Battleplan Different

Plenty of games let you command WW2 armies. Very few let you *draw* the plan. In Battleplan, you sketch multi-stage, multi-unit orders directly on the map — defence in depth, planned withdrawals, counterattacks, preparatory bombardments before a set-piece assault. Then you split divisions to grab multiple objectives and watch your officers execute the intent on their own.

That shift in perspective matters. You're not micromanaging a tank's pathing; you're acting as a corps or army commander issuing intent and trusting subordinates.

| Core System | How It Works | Why It Matters |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Draw-on-map planning | Sketch multi-step orders directly onto terrain | Fast, intuitive way to build complex offensives |
| Realistic order of battle | HQ, regiments, battalions, and companies modeled individually | You manage structure, not just units |
| Officer management | Hire, fire, and keep officers alive for veterancy and quirks | Adds a persistent, human layer to campaigns |
| Real-world geography | Roads, towns, forests, and swamps placed from geographic data | Terrain reading becomes a genuine skill |
| Supply simulation | Terrain dictates supply flow; depots fuel advances | Cutting enemy logistics is a real win condition |
| Time manipulation | Pause, real-time, or fast-forward | You control the pace of a month-long battle |

Missions run from roughly one week to a full month of simulated time, which is a very different rhythm from the 20-minute missions most RTS players are used to. The pause-and-plan loop is essential here — you'll spend far more time thinking than clicking.

## Battleplan News for New Commanders: A Getting-Started Sequence

If you're jumping in, the learning curve is real but manageable. Here's a practical order of operations that matches how the game wants to be played.

| Step | Action | Why It Comes First |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Study the terrain and elevation maps | Artillery placement and supply flow depend on it |
| 2 | Set your chain of command | Officers execute your intent autonomously |
| 3 | Draw a simple, staged plan | Complex plans fail when supply breaks |
| 4 | Secure supply depots early | Captured depots fuel the advance |
| 5 | Use pause liberally | Month-long battles reward deliberation |
| 6 | Pull back when supplies run low | Overextension is the most common self-inflicted loss |

### Drawing Plans Without Overreaching

The temptation is to draw an elaborate, beautiful offensive across the whole map. Resist it. Start with two or three phases: a bombardment, a main thrust, and a consolidation line. Once you understand how your officers interpret orders, you can add feints, planned withdrawals, and secondary objectives.

### Treat Supply as Your Real Enemy

Terrain determines how supply reaches your units, and units that run dry stop being useful. Cutting an enemy's supply lines is often faster than destroying their divisions outright. If you internalize one system from the entire game, make it this one.
