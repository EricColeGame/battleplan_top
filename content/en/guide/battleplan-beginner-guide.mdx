Battleplan hands you an entire division, a map built from real World War II geography, and a single drawing tool — then asks you to win a battle that can last a full month. That sounds intimidating, and it is, which is exactly why a solid Battleplan beginner guide matters. The game launched on October 12, 2026 from developer Foolish Mortals Games and publisher Slitherine, and it simulates huge engagements second by second with hundreds of thousands of troops on the field.

This Battleplan beginner guide will walk you through the command structure, the drawing system, supply management, and the habits that separate a commander who wins in two weeks from one who burns a division in three days. If you have never played a wargame at this scale, start here.

## What Makes Battleplan Different From Other WW2 Strategy Games

Most real-time strategy games put you in the turret. Battleplan puts you in the headquarters tent. You issue high-level orders, then watch your officers and companies carry them out autonomously. You are not micromanaging every rifleman — you are shaping an operational plan and letting your subordinates execute it.

The battlefields are modeled from real-world geographic data, so roads, towns, forests, and swamps sit exactly where they did in real life. That means elevation matters, terrain matters, and the ground you choose to fight on matters more than the size of your force.

| Feature | What It Means for You |
|---|---|
| Scale | Whole divisions, combined into corps or split down to companies |
| Time | Missions run one week to a month, simulated second by second |
| Control | Pause, real-time, or fast-forward at will |
| Planning | Draw multi-step, multi-unit orders directly on the map |
| Maps | Real-world geographic data with authentic elevation |
| Modes | Single-player campaign with Steam Achievements and Cloud saves |
| Price | $29.99 on Steam at launch |

The tag list tells the story: historical, RTS, grand strategy, wargame, time manipulation, simulation. If you are coming from a fast-clicking action RTS, expect an adjustment period.

## Your First Hour: A Step-by-Step Battleplan Beginner Guide

The single biggest mistake new commanders make is unpausing too early. The game lets you sit in pause indefinitely while you plan — use that. Here is a sequence that works.

| Step | Action | Why It Matters |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Read the briefing and study the map before touching anything | Objectives shape everything that follows |
| 2 | Inspect terrain: high ground, roads, rivers, towns | Artillery and supply both follow terrain |
| 3 | Review your order of battle and chain of command | You cannot plan around units you don't know you have |
| 4 | Draw phase lines for your first objective | Multi-step plans beat single arrows |
| 5 | Schedule preparatory bombardment before the main attack | Softens defenders before infantry moves |
| 6 | Check supply routes into your starting positions | An unsupplied attack stalls fast |
| 7 | Run at fast-forward, pause on first contact | React to reality, not to your assumptions |
| 8 | Reassign or split units to capture secondary objectives | One division can take multiple points if split well |

Once your first plan is drawn, resist the urge to redraw it every thirty seconds. Give your officers time to execute. Their autonomy is the core of the design, and constant interference usually produces worse results than a clean, well-sequenced order.

## Understanding the Chain of Command and Order of Battle

Each division is modeled with a headquarters, regiments, battalions, and companies. You can customize the entire chain of command, combine divisions into corps, or split units all the way down to companies. Requisition and upgrade units as the campaign progresses, and hire or fire officers as you see fit.

| Level | Typical Role | What You Decide |
|---|---|---|
| Corps | Groups multiple divisions | Frontage, priority of effort |
| Division | Main maneuver unit | Objectives, tempo, attachments |
| Regiment | Brigade-level control | Sector responsibility |
| Battalion | Tactical fighting force | Assault or hold orders |
| Company | Smallest maneuver element | Splitting for multiple objectives |

Officers matter more than most newcomers expect. Keep them alive and they gain veterancy — along with quirks that may help or hurt. They also offer advice. You can listen, or ignore them entirely, but a veteran officer with a good read on the ground is often worth hearing out before you commit to a risky flank.

Unit types work together rather than alone: infantry, tanks, engineers, anti-tank guns, artillery, and scouts each solve a problem the others cannot. Engineers clear obstacles, scouts find the enemy, anti-tank guns stop armor, and artillery shapes the battlefield before anyone moves.

## Logistics, Supply, and Terrain: The Systems That Win Battles

Supply is realistically simulated, and terrain dictates how it flows. You can starve an enemy by cutting their supply lines, and you can fuel your own advance by capturing supply depots. When your units run low, pulling back is not cowardice — it is the correct play.

| Supply Factor | Effect on Your Battle |
|---|---|
| Terrain | Determines how quickly supply reaches the front |
| Depots | Capturing them refuels your advance |
| Cut lines | Starving the enemy stalls their attacks |
| Distance | Longer advances stretch your supply further |
| Retreat timing | Pulling back early preserves combat power |

A useful rule of thumb for any Battleplan beginner guide reader: plan your offensive in phases, and treat each phase as a supply checkpoint. If phase one takes longer than expected, your phase two units may already be running thin. Watching supply is often more valuable than watching the front line.

## Common Beginner Mistakes and How to Fix Them

Community reports from early players, and the game's mixed reception on Steam at launch, suggest that most frustration comes from a handful of repeatable errors rather than from the game being unfair. Player experience points to these fixes.

| Mistake | Why It Hurts | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Never pausing | Plans get drawn in a panic | Plan in pause, execute in real time |
| Single-arrow attacks | Predictable, easy to blunt | Use multi-step, multi-unit plans |
| Ignoring logistics | Attacks stall mid-push | Check supply before every phase |
| Over-controlling units | Officers perform worse when micromanaged | Set intent, then let them work |
| Letting officers die | Lose veterancy and quirks permanently | Rotate exhausted commanders out |
| Skipping terrain study | Artillery placed badly, flanks exposed | Use elevation maps before deploying |
| Splitting too early | Nothing achieves decisive mass | Concentrate, then split for objectives |

Tactics worth learning early include defense in depth, planned withdrawals, and counterattacks. A planned withdrawal that pulls an overextended enemy into a prepared kill zone is often stronger than holding ground you cannot supply.

## FAQ

**Is Battleplan hard for a complete beginner?**
It has a learning curve, but it is more forgiving than it looks because you can pause indefinitely while planning. This Battleplan beginner guide's core advice — plan in pause, execute in real time — removes most of the pressure. Expect your first mission to go badly and your third to go much better.

**Do I need to micromanage individual units?**
No. The design deliberately pushes you toward high-level orders. You set intent, and your officers and companies carry it out autonomously. Micromanaging usually produces worse results than a clear plan.

**What is the most important system to learn first?**
Supply. Terrain dictates how supply flows, and an advance without supply stalls. Learning to capture depots and pull back when units run low will improve your results faster than any tactical trick.

**How long does a single mission take?**
Missions are simulated second by second and range from roughly one week to a month of in-game time. Because you can fast-forward at will, a full mission can be played in a single sitting once you know what you are doing.

Get the fundamentals right — pause, plan, supply, and let your officers work — and Battleplan stops feeling like chaos and starts feeling like command.
