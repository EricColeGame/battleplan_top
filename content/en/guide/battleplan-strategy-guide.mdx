## The Core Command Loop: Draw, Delegate, Adjust

You do not issue individual movement orders. You draw on the map. Multi-step, multi-unit plans are sketched directly onto the terrain, and then your officers and companies interpret them autonomously. That hand-off is the heart of the game, and it is also where new players lose campaigns.

The loop has three beats:

1. **Draw** — sketch the offensive, the defensive line, the withdrawal route, or the preparatory bombardment.
2. **Delegate** — let the chain of command carry the plan down to battalions and companies.
3. **Adjust** — watch what actually happens at contact, then redraw rather than micromanage.

Because every mission is simulated in real time, you choose your own tempo. Player experience suggests the biggest early improvement is learning when *not* to intervene.

| Control mode | Best used for | Watch out for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Pause | Drawing multi-stage plans, checking supply overlays, swapping officers | Over-planning; orders stacking faster than units can execute them |
| Real time | Judging whether a drawn plan holds once contact begins | Reacting too late to a flank that is quietly collapsing |
| Fast-forward | Quiet marching phases and waiting for supply to catch up | Skipping past the moment a breakthrough needed a decision |

## Building an Order of Battle That Holds Together

Every division is modeled with a real structure: headquarters, regiments, battalions, and companies. You can combine divisions into corps, or split them down to company level. Requisition and upgrade units as the campaign progresses. The temptation is to build the biggest stack possible and push it forward. The better habit is to build formations that match the ground they are crossing.

| Level | Role | What you actually decide |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Corps | Groups several divisions under one command | Which division carries the main effort |
| Division | The main maneuver formation | Objectives, tempo, whether it splits |
| Regiment | Mid-level grouping within a division | Which axis of advance |
| Battalion | The workhorse for one task | Hold, attack, screen, or pursue |
| Company | The smallest element you can detach | Grabbing a bridge, town, or crossroads |

### When to Split a Division

Splitting a division to capture multiple objectives at once is one of the strongest tools available, but it is not free.

- **Split when** the objectives are close, the enemy is already broken, and supply lines are short.
- **Split when** you need a small force to screen a flank while the main body pushes.
- **Do not split** to chase two distant objectives with one division — you will arrive everywhere in insufficient strength.
- **Do not split** right before a set-piece attack; concentrate for the preparatory bombardment, then divide afterward.

The composition of each formation matters too. Infantry, tanks, engineers, anti-tank guns, artillery, and scouts all behave differently and cooperate on their own. A division without engineers will struggle at rivers and fortifications; a division without anti-tank guns will regret meeting armor on open ground.

## Officers, Veterancy, and the Human Element

Officers are not stat blocks you can ignore. You hire and fire them, listen to their advice or dismiss it, and — most importantly — try to keep them alive. Officers who survive gain veterancy, and veterans develop quirks alongside their competence. That trade-off is one of the most interesting systems in the game.

| Decision | Upside | Downside |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Keep a veteran in command | Reliable execution, quirks you already understand | Painful to replace if killed |
| Promote a fresh officer | Cheap, and sometimes sharper local advice | Less steady under pressure |
| Follow staff advice | Exploits a local opportunity you missed | Can fragment your overall plan |
| Ignore staff advice | Keeps your plan coherent | You may overlook a genuine opening |

A workable rule: follow advice that reinforces your plan's intent, and ignore advice that would pull a formation off its axis of advance. Also rotate officers out of the most exposed headquarters positions once they have accumulated veterancy — losing a division commander mid-offensive costs more than a few hours of staff churn.

## Logistics and Terrain: The Quiet Deciders

Supply is simulated realistically, and terrain dictates how it flows. Roads matter. Swamps matter. A division that outruns its supply line is a division that stops moving, no matter how good the plan drawn on the map looked.

| Priority | Action | Why it matters |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Secure supply depots on your axis of advance | Fuel keeps the offensive moving |
| 2 | Cut enemy supply lines before a frontal assault | Starving a defender is cheaper than grinding through them |
| 3 | Pull back when units run low | Recovers strength instead of losing it to attrition |
| 4 | Check elevation before placing artillery | Real-world elevation maps reward high ground |
| 5 | Re-read the ground after every redraw | Roads, towns, forests, and swamps sit exactly where they do in real life |

The battlefields are built from real-world geographic data, spanning thousands of kilometers. That means the terrain is not decoration — it is the constraint that decides which plans are even possible. Before you draw an offensive, look at where the roads run and where the elevation sits.

There is a historical layer to this as well: geo-tagged photographs and video are placed where they were actually taken, so the map doubles as a way to learn the real ground you are fighting over.

## Battleplan Strategy Guide: Mistakes to Avoid and Habits to Build

Community reports from the launch window show a recognizable pattern. Players who bounce off the game tend to be fighting the design rather than playing it. This Battleplan strategy guide's short list of fixes:

| Mistake | Symptom | Fix |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Micromanaging companies | Constant redrawing, exhausted attention | Write one clear multi-stage plan and let officers run it |
| Ignoring supply | Offensive stalls two-thirds of the way to the objective | Track depots and pull back before units run dry |
| Splitting too early | Every objective attacked with too little force | Concentrate, bombard, then split |
| Chasing objectives without terrain | Units bog down in swamps and forests | Route along roads and use elevation |
| Discarding officers | No veterancy in the chain of command | Protect veterans; rotate them out of the hottest HQs |
| Playing only at fast-forward | Missing the decisive moment | Slow down whenever contact is imminent |

Habits worth building instead:

- **Plan in phases.** A preparatory bombardment, a main assault, and a follow-up exploitation are three separate orders, not one.
- **Keep a reserve.** Defence in depth and planned withdrawals only work if something is left uncommitted.
- **Redraw, don't panic.** Adjusting the drawn plan is faster than manually herding battalions.
- **Read the review landscape critically.** The game launched to a mixed reception, with roughly two-thirds of early reviews positive. Much of the criticism centers on the hands-off command style — which is precisely the feature the design is built around.

## FAQ

**Is Battleplan a strategy game or a wargame simulation?**
It is a historical World War II real-time strategy game built around simulation. You command entire divisions, customize your order of battle and chain of command, and watch companies carry out your drawn plans autonomously.

**How long does a single Battleplan mission take?**
Missions range from about one week to one month of simulated time, played out second by second. You can pause, run at normal speed, or fast-forward depending on how much is happening.

**Do I need to control individual squads in Battleplan?**
No. That is the point of the design. Your job is to draw the plan and manage the chain of command; infantry, tanks, engineers, artillery, and scouts handle their own tasks once ordered.

**What should a new player focus on first?**
Logistics and terrain. Anyone reading a Battleplan strategy guide for the first time tends to focus on tactics, but supply flow and elevation decide more battles than clever flanking does. Secure depots, keep your officers alive, and let the plan do the work.
