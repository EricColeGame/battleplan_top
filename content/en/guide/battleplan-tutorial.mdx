## Why Battleplan Plays Differently From Other WW2 RTS Games

Battleplan drops you into month-long WW2 campaigns where hundreds of thousands of troops move in real time — and it expects you to command divisions, not individual squads. That scale is exactly why a solid Battleplan tutorial matters: the systems that decide who wins, including map-drawn orders, supply flow, and officer autonomy, are the systems the game explains the least. This Battleplan tutorial walks you through the core loop, the drawing tools, and the logistics rules so your first campaign doesn't collapse inside a week.

The game launched on October 12, 2026, developed by Foolish Mortals Games Inc. and published by Slitherine Ltd. It's a single-player title priced at $29.99, and it's built around one unusual promise: you issue high-level orders, then watch your subordinates carry them out on their own. ## The Battleplan Tutorial Core Loop: Pause, Draw, Execute

Every mission follows the same rhythm: read the map, draw a plan, watch it unfold, then adjust. The campaign missions introduce this loop gradually, but they don't stress how much the time controls shape your decisions. In practice, strong players spend most of their time paused.

| Mode | Best used for | Risk if you overuse it |
|---|---|---|
| Paused | Drawing multi-step orders, checking supply overlays, comparing terrain | Analysis paralysis — the clock stops, but the campaign doesn't |
| Real time | Reacting to breakthroughs, committing reserves, watching an assault develop | Orders issued a minute late may land after the moment has passed |
| Fast-forward | Quiet stretches, waiting on preparatory bombardment, letting supply build | Overshooting a critical window without noticing |

A practical rule of thumb: pause when you plan, run at real time when you react, and fast-forward only when you're confident nothing is about to change. If you're new, treat fast-forward as a reward for a well-drawn plan rather than your default speed.

The second half of the loop is knowing what level to command at. You control divisions and corps; the simulation handles companies. If you catch yourself trying to nudge a single platoon across a field, you've dropped below the level the game was designed for, and you'll lose the bigger picture while doing it.

## Drawing Orders on the Map: A Step-by-Step Walkthrough

The signature mechanic in Battleplan is drawing directly onto the map. Instead of selecting a unit and clicking a destination, you sketch intent — axes of advance, defensive lines, phased objectives, and withdrawal routes. The order then flows down through your chain of command.

| Step | What to draw or set | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Recon the ground | Inspect elevation, roads, forests, towns, and swamps | Terrain governs movement speed and supply flow |
| 2. Mark objectives | Place the first objective on a road junction, depot, or town | Objectives that matter are usually terrain, not enemy units |
| 3. Sketch the approach | Draw an axis of advance for each formation | Gives subordinates a clear direction of attack |
| 4. Add phases | Layer a second and third stage: exploitation, then consolidation | Multi-step plans keep momentum after the first objective falls |
| 5. Plan the fires | Schedule preparatory bombardment before set-piece attacks | Suppressed defenders break faster and cost you fewer troops |
| 6. Define the fallback | Draw a defensive line or withdrawal route behind the advance | Planned withdrawals beat panicked ones every time |

Two habits separate clean plans from messy ones. First, split a division only when you genuinely need to take multiple objectives at once — splitting early thins your combat power and complicates supply. Second, build in defence in depth. A second line behind your lead regiment absorbs counterattacks without forcing you to redraw everything mid-battle.

Community reports consistently suggest that the players who struggle most are the ones who draw one arrow and expect it to finish the battle. Phasing is not optional in this game.

## Chain of Command, Unit Roles, and Officer Management

Battleplan models each division realistically, with a headquarters, regiments, battalions, and companies. Infantry, tanks, engineers, anti-tank guns, artillery, and scouts all work together and perform tasks autonomously, which means your job is to put the right tools in the right places.

| Unit type | Primary job | Watch out for |
|---|---|---|
| Infantry | Hold ground, clear towns and forests | Slow going without artillery support |
| Tanks | Break through and exploit gaps | Heavy fuel and supply demands |
| Engineers | Reduce obstacles, fortifications, and bridges | Thin and vulnerable if left in the front line |
| Anti-tank guns | Blunt armored counterattacks | Must be sited before the tanks arrive |
| Artillery | Prep bombardments, suppress, break up attacks | Ammo consumption; place on high ground |
| Scouts | Reveal ground and locate the enemy | Not a fighting force — don't spend them |

You can customize your entire order of battle, combining divisions into corps or splitting them down to companies, and requisition or upgrade units as the campaign progresses. Officers are the other half of that equation. You can hire and fire them, take their advice or ignore it, and keep them alive so they accumulate veterancy — plus quirks that, per player experience, can cut either way. Rotating a headquarters away from the front line is rarely the wrong call.

## Supply, Terrain, and the Mistakes That Break New Commanders

Supply is the quiet system that decides most campaigns. Terrain dictates how supply flows, so a road through a swamp is not the same lifeline as a road through open farmland. Cutting enemy supply lines is often more efficient than destroying enemy units outright.

| Symptom | Likely cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Advance stalls mid-attack | Supply line stretched beyond what the terrain can carry | Pause the push and consolidate on a depot |
| Artillery goes silent | Ammo isn't reaching the battery | Capture a closer depot or reposition the guns |
| Units slow down unexpectedly | Roads cut behind you, or you're crossing marsh | Reroute or shorten the line before continuing |
| Counterattack hits hard | You outran your anti-tank coverage | Site guns on the reverse slope before moving on |
| Whole formation starving | Enemy is raiding your supply flow | Detach a covering force to guard the route |

### Beginner Mistakes Worth Avoiding

| Mistake | Why it hurts | Better habit |
|---|---|---|
| Micromanaging companies | Pulls your attention below the level the game models | Set intent and let officers execute |
| Attacking without prep fire | Defenders in cover trade far too well | Always schedule bombardment first |
| Chasing every objective at once | Splits combat power and supply | Sequence objectives and finish them |
| Ignoring elevation | Artillery and observation both suffer | Put guns and observers on high ground |
| Letting officers die | You lose veterancy and unit cohesion | Keep headquarters out of the direct fight |
| Fast-forwarding entire battles | You miss narrow reaction windows | Save fast-forward for quiet stretches |

One last piece of advice for anyone working through a Battleplan tutorial: treat your first campaign as a learning run. The game's mixed reception at launch — around two-thirds positive across the first several dozen Steam reviews — largely reflects how unforgiving the simulation can feel before the supply and command systems click. Once they do, the scale becomes the appeal rather than the obstacle.

## Battleplan Tutorial FAQ

**Does Battleplan have a dedicated tutorial mode?**
It teaches through its campaign missions rather than a separate hand-holding mode, which is why a written Battleplan tutorial like this one is useful. Treat your opening missions as practice: experiment with the drawing tools, pause constantly, and don't worry about a perfect result.

**How long does a single mission take?**
Missions simulate anywhere from one week to a month of in-game time, running second by second. You can pause, play in real time, or fast-forward at will, so the wall-clock length depends entirely on how you use those controls.

**Do I need to micromanage individual units?**
No — and you probably shouldn't. You give high-level orders and your officers and companies carry them out autonomously. Your attention is better spent on objectives, phasing, terrain, and supply.

**What should I learn first?**
Prep fire and supply. Scheduling a bombardment before a set-piece attack and keeping your line short enough to sustain will improve your results faster than any other single habit.
