Most WW2 strategy games hand you a sandbox full of units and let you click until something breaks. **Battleplan Normandy** flips that loop: you draw the operation directly onto the map, then watch your officers carry it out — or fumble it. That single design decision turns a familiar setting into a command problem rather than a clicking contest.

**Battleplan Normandy** is the shorthand players use for the WW2 operational experience in *Battleplan*, a historical real-time strategy game from developer Foolish Mortals Games Inc. and publisher Slitherine Ltd. It launched on October 12, 2026, at $29.99, and it is built around one ambitious promise: simulate enormous, month-long battles — hundreds of thousands of troops — second by second, while you stay at the operational level. Below, we break down how the game works, what its maps and armies actually model, and what the early reviews say.

## Why Battleplan Normandy Stands Out in the WW2 Strategy Genre

Most real-time strategy games put you in a tank or behind a squad. Battleplan Normandy keeps your boots at division level and above. You are not micro-managing individual soldiers; you are writing the plan, assigning the commanders, and then living with the consequences as the simulation unfolds.

That is a meaningful departure from the genre's habits. Where a typical RTS rewards fast clicking and unit micro, this one rewards anticipation: reading terrain, guessing where the enemy will mass, and building a plan that still works when the first stage goes wrong. The game's own framing calls it a simulation-first wargame, and the popular tags around it — historical, wargame, grand strategy, real time tactics — point to the same audience.

The core design pillars are worth listing up front, because they explain almost every review you will read:

- **Plans drawn by hand** on the map, not issued through menus
- **Autonomous subordinates** who execute your orders without babysitting
- **Real-world geography**, including elevation, roads, and water
- **Logistics as a weapon** — supply lines matter as much as firepower
- **Time control**, so you can pause, play live, or fast-forward

## How Battleplan Normandy Rebuilds Real Terrain

The battlefields are the headline feature. Rather than sculpting fictional maps, the developers built them from real-world geographic data, covering thousands of kilometers. Roads, towns, forests, and swamps sit where they sit in real life, and elevation is modeled closely enough that you can scout high ground and site your artillery accordingly.

That approach is why the Normandy campaign is such a natural lens for the game. The 1944 fighting in northern France punished exactly the things this game simulates: dense bocage that swallowed armor, flooded marshland that guarded flanks for free, and a supply race that decided how fast an advance could actually move.

If you know the history, you already understand the map — and that is the point.

| Terrain feature | Historical role in Normandy | How you use it in Battleplan Normandy |
|---|---|---|
| Bocage hedgerows | Slowed Allied advances for weeks | Funnel attacks along field boundaries instead of pushing frontally |
| Flooded marshes | Protected the Cotentin Peninsula's flanks | Treat as a free flank guard; do not waste units there |
| Ridges and high ground | Artillery observation points | Site guns and observers on elevation |
| Road junctions and towns | Supply choke points | Capture to fuel your advance, cut to starve the enemy |
| Rivers and bridges | Breakout bottlenecks | Attach engineers early and plan bridging before you need it |

Terrain also drives supply. The game simulates supply flow realistically, with the ground itself shaping how quickly and where resources reach your forward units. A road network you ignore today is a stalled offensive next week.

## Drawing the Plan: How Command Works in Battleplan Normandy

This is the feature that separates Battleplan Normandy from almost everything else in the genre. Instead of selecting a unit and right-clicking a target, you sketch multi-step, multi-unit plans straight onto the map. Arrows, phases, objectives — the whole operation exists as a drawing before a single shot is fired.

Orders then flow down the chain of command. You issue the intent; your officers and companies figure out the details and execute autonomously. That means a good plan with mediocre units usually beats a bad plan with excellent ones.

| Plan type | What it does | When to use it |
|---|---|---|
| Preparatory bombardment | Softens a position before a set-piece attack | Before assaulting a fortified town or ridge |
| Defence in depth | Layered positions that absorb an attack | When you are outnumbered and buying time |
| Counterattack | A timed strike after the enemy overextends | After an armored push stalls out |
| Planned withdrawal | An ordered pullback to a shorter line | When your units are running low on supply |
| Multi-objective split | Divides a division to seize several targets | When objectives are spread across a wide front |

Time is fully under your control. Every mission is simulated second by second, and you can pause, run it in real time, or fast-forward. Since missions can span a week to a month of in-game time, that flexibility is not a luxury — it is the only way to manage a campaign without losing your evening.

A practical tip from the design itself: plan in phases and leave yourself an exit. The game explicitly supports planned withdrawals and defence in depth, which means a retreat executed on your terms is a tactic, not a failure.

## Army Building and Logistics in Battleplan Normandy

Your army is modeled down to a granular level. Each division contains a headquarters, regiments, battalions, and companies, and you can reorganize freely — merging divisions into corps or splitting them down to company level for a specific job. Throughout a campaign you requisition new units, upgrade existing ones, and hire or dismiss the officers who lead them.

Officers are not cosmetic. They offer advice you can accept or ignore, they gain veterancy the longer they survive, and they pick up quirks along the way. Losing a seasoned commander costs you more than the unit he led, so protecting your leadership is a real strategic constraint.

| Unit type | Primary job | Watch out for |
|---|---|---|
| Infantry | Hold ground, clear towns and hedgerows | Fatigue and steady supply drain |
| Tanks | Breakthrough and exploitation | Fuel consumption; poor in close terrain |
| Engineers | Bridging, obstacle clearing, assault support | Vulnerable without infantry cover |
| Anti-tank guns | Ambush armor on roads and approaches | Slow to reposition once emplaced |
| Artillery | Preparatory fires, counter-battery, interdiction | Needs observation and ammunition |
| Scouts | Reconnaissance and screening | Very low combat power |

Logistics ties it all together. You can starve an enemy by cutting their supply lines, fuel your own advance by seizing depots, and — crucially — pull back when your units run dry. In practice, the player who manages supply usually beats the player who manages firepower, which is a lesson the Normandy campaign taught the hard way in 1944.

On reception, the picture is genuinely mixed. Community reports at the time of writing put the game at a "Mixed" overall rating, with roughly two-thirds of 63 user reviews positive. Because that sample is small, the fairest reading is that the Mixed label signals a niche, simulation-first design rather than a verdict on quality — players who want hands-off command and real geography tend to click with it, while players expecting a cinematic, squad-level campaign may bounce off.

A few practical notes for newcomers, drawn from how the systems are designed:

- **Learn supply before you learn tactics.** Cutting a road often does more than winning a firefight.
- **Use the pause freely.** Planning is the game; reaction speed is not.
- **Keep your officers alive.** Veterancy compounds over a long campaign.
- **Study elevation maps.** High ground is artillery advantage, and it is modeled from real data.
- **Use the history layer.** The game includes geo-tagged historical photos and videos placed where they were actually taken, which doubles as a genuinely useful learning tool.

## Battleplan Normandy FAQ

**What is Battleplan Normandy?**
It is the common shorthand for the WW2 operational experience in *Battleplan*, a historical real-time strategy game published by Slitherine Ltd. and developed by Foolish Mortals Games Inc. You command divisions and corps, draw plans directly on real-world maps, and watch your officers execute them.

**Is Battleplan Normandy single-player only?**
Yes. The game ships as a single-player experience, with Steam Achievements, Steam Cloud support, and Family Sharing. There is no advertised multiplayer mode.

**How long does a single mission take?**
Missions simulate between roughly one week and one month of in-game time, running second by second. You can pause, play in real time, or fast-forward at will, so a full operation can be played in a single sitting or spread across several.

**Do I need to know WW2 history to enjoy it?**
No, but it helps. The game includes geo-tagged historical photos and videos placed at their real locations, so it works as a learning tool as well as a wargame. Understanding the terrain problems of 1944 — bocage, marshes, and supply bottlenecks — will make your plans noticeably better.

**What are the system requirements?**
At minimum, a 64-bit Windows 10 or 11 machine with 4 GB of RAM, a DirectX 11-compatible graphics card, and 8 GB of free storage. The recommended configuration calls for a modern 4-core processor and 8 GB of RAM.

Battleplan Normandy will not appeal to everyone — its hands-off command model and its insistence on real geography make it a deliberate, planning-heavy game. But for players who have ever looked at a WW2 map and wanted to draw arrows on it, that is exactly the point.
