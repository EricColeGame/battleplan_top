## What Battleplan Army Command Really Means

Battleplan is not a game about dragging individual squads behind cover. It is a game about thinking like a corps commander. In this WW2 real-time strategy title from Foolish Mortals Games and Slitherine, you take charge of entire divisions on battlefields rebuilt from real-world geographic data — and the Battleplan army command system rewards planning far more than reflexes. That matters because a single mission can simulate a week to a month of fighting, second by second, with hundreds of thousands of troops in motion. If you try to steer every company by hand, you will lose. If you learn how Battleplan army command actually flows — from the plans you draw, down through your officers, to the companies that execute them — you can win fights that look unwinnable on paper.

The scale here is the whole point. The [Battleplan store page on Steam](https://store.steampowered.com/app/3743140/Battleplan/) describes a simulation where you give high-level orders and then watch your officers and companies carry them out autonomously. That is not a marketing flourish — it is the core loop, and everything else in the game is built around it.

### Command Echelons at a Glance

| Echelon | What it represents | Your level of control |
|---|---|---|
| Corps | Two or more divisions grouped for a major offensive | Assign broad objectives; shift whole formations between pushes |
| Division | Your primary maneuver unit | Draw plans, set objectives, allocate support |
| Regiment | Mid-level HQ that translates division intent | Mostly autonomous; inspect and adjust when needed |
| Battalion | Combined-arms building block: infantry, armor, engineers, artillery | Managed through requisitions and upgrades |
| Company | The smallest autonomous element | Executes tasks; splits off for multi-objective attacks |

The practical takeaway is simple: almost all of your clicking happens at the division level and above. Everything below that is a system you influence, not a unit you steer. Once that clicks, the game stops feeling unresponsive and starts feeling like delegation.

## Building Your Chain of Command: Officers, Veterancy, and Order of Battle

Each division is modeled with a headquarters, regiments, battalions, and companies. You customize the chain of command and the order of battle for your entire army — combining divisions into corps when a push needs weight, or splitting units down to companies when a mission demands wide coverage. Everything in Battleplan army command starts here, because a poorly organized formation will execute your brilliant plan badly.

### Why Officers Matter More Than You Think

Officers are not decoration. A division HQ led by an experienced officer handles complicated, multi-step orders more reliably. Keep your officers alive and they gain veterancy — along with quirks that can cut both ways. You can hire and fire freely, and their advice is essentially free reconnaissance: useful, but not always correct.

| Officer action | What it does | Trade-off |
|---|---|---|
| Hire an officer | Fills a division HQ slot | Cheap and available, but untested |
| Keep a veteran alive | Builds veterancy and quirks over a campaign | Veterans are hard to replace |
| Fire an officer | Opens the slot for someone new | Disruption; the replacement starts fresh |
| Heed advice | Surfaces local intel and suggestions | Advice can be wrong — verify with scouts |
| Reorganize the order of battle | Moves units between HQs | Takes time; formations may need to re-consolidate |

### Requisitions and Upgrades

Units can be requisitioned and upgraded throughout a campaign, so match your purchases to the ground you expect to fight on. Anti-tank guns belong on open approaches, engineers belong where you need to break fortified lines, and scouts belong anywhere the map is wide and your flanks are exposed. Buying the right tools for the terrain is half of good command.

## Drawing Multi-Stage Plans on the Map

Drawing directly on the map is the part of Battleplan army command that gets the most attention, and it deserves it. You sketch multi-step, multi-unit plans intuitively, then chain phases together: bombard, assault, split, hold. The game supports defense in depth, counterattacks, planned withdrawals, and more — and the sequencing is what separates a frontal slog from a collapse.

| Plan or tactic | When to use it | Command consideration |
|---|---|---|
| Preparatory bombardment | Before a set-piece attack | Costs time and supply; soften the line before infantry moves |
| Defense in depth | Absorbing a heavy enemy offensive | Trade ground for time; keep reserves uncommitted |
| Counterattack | After an enemy push runs out of steam | Requires a fresh, fully supplied formation |
| Planned withdrawal | When a flank is about to collapse | Preserves units; waiting too long turns it into a rout |
| Multi-objective split | Capturing several points at once | Each detachment is weaker — only split if you have the numbers |

Think about what a single order actually produces. Telling a division to "take the town" gives you a frontal assault with predictable casualties. A three-stage plan that bombards the defenses, breaches at one point, and then splits to cut the roads behind the town produces something much better: an enemy that cannot retreat and cannot resupply. Same division, same troops, dramatically different result — purely from how you drew the plan.

## Supply, Terrain, and Time: The Layers Beneath Your Orders

No plan survives contact with an empty fuel tank. Supply is simulated realistically in Battleplan, and terrain dictates how it flows to your units. Battleplan army command is therefore as much about roads and depots as it is about arrows on a map.

| Supply factor | Effect | Practical tip |
|---|---|---|
| Supply depots | Fuel your advance | Capture them before you push deep, not after |
| Enemy supply lines | Starving the enemy weakens resistance | Raid them with fast units and scouts |
| Terrain | Roads speed supply; swamps and forests slow it | Plan advances along road networks |
| Low supply | Units slow down, then stall | Pull back before you are fully committed |
| High ground | Better artillery positions | Check real elevation data before placing guns |

Time control is the other layer, and it is easy to underuse. You can pause, play in real time, or fast-forward at will — and each mode has a specific job.

| Time control | Best used for | Why it helps |
|---|---|---|
| Pause | Drawing and revising multi-stage plans | Lets you think without losing ground |
| Real time | Watching an offensive unfold | Lets you react to breakthroughs and collapses |
| Fast-forward | Waiting out bombardments and slow supply recovery | Saves minutes without losing awareness |

A practical rhythm for Battleplan army command: pause to plan, run at normal speed while your lead formation makes contact, then fast-forward through the quiet stretches. Speeding through contact is exactly how you miss the moment an enemy flank folds — and that moment is usually where the battle is decided.

## Five Command Mistakes That Lose Campaigns

Steam user reviews for Battleplan currently sit at a "Mixed" rating, with roughly two-thirds of early reviews positive (community reports at the time of writing). Reading through player feedback, the same handful of command errors come up again and again.

| Mistake | Symptom | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Micromanaging companies | Orders overwrite each other; units stall | Set intent and let officers execute |
| Attacking without preparation | Set-piece attacks bleed out | Bombard first, then assault |
| Outrunning supply | The advance stalls mid-push | Take depots and pause on roads |
| Ignoring officer advice entirely | You miss local intel | Read it, then verify with scouts |
| Committing reserves too early | Nothing left to answer a counterattack | Hold one formation back |

A few habits that fix most of these problems:

- **Read the terrain before you draw.** High ground for artillery, roads for logistics, forests for cover. Real-world elevation data is right there in the game.
- **Protect your officers.** Veterancy compounds over a campaign, and a dead HQ officer sets a division back for weeks of simulated time.
- **Keep one formation fresh.** Uncommitted reserves are what turn a successful defense into a decisive counterattack.
- **Use the pause button generously.** It costs nothing and routinely saves divisions.

The through-line is that Battleplan army command is about intent, not input. Players who fight the simulation lose to it; players who work with it — planning in phases, feeding supply, trusting their officers — get results that look almost effortless.

## FAQ

**Do I have to micromanage individual units in Battleplan army command?**
No. You set high-level objectives and your officers and companies carry them out autonomously. You influence the fight through plans, formations, supply, and officer quality rather than direct control of every squad.

**How do I keep my officers alive and level them up?**
Keep division headquarters out of the front line, rotate battered formations out before they break, and avoid throwing HQ units into close assaults. Officers who survive a campaign gain veterancy — and quirks that add personality and risk.

**What is the fastest way to learn the command system?**
Start with a single division, draw one two-stage plan per mission, and watch how your companies execute it. Once you can predict where a plan will stall, add a second division and practice coordinating corps-level pushes.

**Can I reorganize my army in the middle of a campaign?**
Yes. You can combine divisions into corps, split units down to companies, requisition and upgrade units, and hire or fire officers as the campaign develops. Reorganization costs time, so do it during quiet stretches rather than in the middle of an offensive.
