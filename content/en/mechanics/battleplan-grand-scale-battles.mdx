Most WW2 strategy games hand you a few squads and a map the size of a football pitch. Battleplan grand scale battles work the other way around: you command entire divisions across hundreds of kilometers of real terrain, and the fighting plays out second by second for weeks at a time. That shift in scope changes everything about how you think, plan, and react. If you have ever wanted to feel like a corps commander rather than a squad leader, Battleplan grand scale battles are the closest a real-time strategy game has come to that fantasy — and they demand a very different set of habits.

This guide breaks down how the scale actually works in practice: what you control, what your officers handle for you, and which systems decide whether your offensive rolls forward or stalls in a swamp three days from the start line.

## What Makes Battleplan Grand Scale Battles Different

Battleplan released on October 12, 2026, developed by Foolish Mortals Games Inc. and published by Slitherine Ltd. It is a single-player, historical WW2 real-time strategy game, and its entire pitch rests on operational command: you run divisions, not fireteams. You write the plan; your officers carry it out.

| Element | Typical WW2 RTS | Battleplan |
|---|---|---|
| Unit scale | Squads and single vehicles | Divisions, split down to companies |
| Map size | One battle map | Thousands of kilometers of real geography |
| Mission length | 20–60 minutes | Roughly one week to one month |
| Order style | Click-to-move micro | Drawn, multi-stage, multi-unit plans |
| Logistics | Abstracted or ignored | Simulated, terrain-driven supply |
| Time control | Real-time only | Pause, real-time, and fast-forward |

The store page lists the game at $29.99, with English full audio and subtitle support for French, German, Spanish, Polish, and Russian. It ships as a single-player experience with Steam Achievements, Cloud saves, and Family Sharing.

That table explains the appeal in one glance. Battleplan grand scale battles are not about reaction speed — they are about anticipating where a front will bend three days before it actually does.

## Real Geography, Real Terrain, Real Consequences

Every road, town, forest, and swamp sits where it does in real life, because the battlefields are built from real-world geographic data. Elevation is modeled too, and it matters more than most new commanders expect: artillery on high ground sees farther and survives longer than artillery parked in a valley.

| Terrain | Effect on play | How to exploit it |
|---|---|---|
| Ridges and high ground | Better observation and artillery reach | Anchor guns and observation posts here |
| Towns | Dense cover plus road junctions | Fight for them as staging and supply hubs |
| Forests | Concealment, slower movement | Stage counterattacks; avoid blind advances |
| Swamps | Severely slows vehicles | Treat as a flank guard, never a highway |
| Roads | Fast movement, easy interdiction | Move quickly but expect to be bombed |

Because the terrain is drawn from real data, a single muddy corridor can throttle an entire corps. Community reports consistently point to the same beginner mistake: committing armor before checking the elevation and swamp layers. Spend five minutes on the map layer before you spend five hours fighting over ground you cannot hold.

The history angle is a bonus rather than a gimmick. Geo-tagged historical photos and video clips appear where they were originally taken, so pushing a division through a real village can double as a genuine history lesson. That is unusual for the genre, and it reinforces why Battleplan grand scale battles feel grounded rather than abstract.

## Drawing the Plan: Orders That Flow Down the Chain of Command

The signature mechanic is drawing. You sketch multi-step, multi-unit plans directly onto the map, then watch them execute. That includes defence in depth, planned withdrawals, counterattacks, and preparatory bombardments before a set-piece assault.

| Tactic | What it looks like | Best used when |
|---|---|---|
| Preparatory bombardment | Timed artillery before the assault | You need to soften a fortified line |
| Defence in depth | Layered fallback positions | You expect to be outnumbered |
| Planned withdrawal | Deliberate pullback to a stronger line | Your flank is about to collapse |
| Counterattack | Reserves striking a spent attacker | The enemy has outrun their supply |
| Multi-objective split | Dividing a division across targets | You hold local superiority |

Every division is modeled with headquarters, regiments, battalions, and companies. The different arms — infantry, tanks, engineers, anti-tank guns, artillery, and scouts — cooperate autonomously once the plan is set. You can combine divisions into corps or break them down to companies, requisition and upgrade units through the campaign, and hire or fire officers as you see fit.

Officers are worth managing carefully. Keep them alive and they gain veterancy, but they also develop quirks that cut both ways. Their advice is usually worth reading, though not always worth following — and that tension is exactly what makes Battleplan grand scale battles feel like command rather than clicking.

## Supply Wins the War Before the Shooting Starts

Logistics is a first-class system here rather than a bar you top up between fights. Cut an enemy's supply line and their advance starves. Capture depots and your own push keeps rolling. Terrain shapes how supply flows, so a bottleneck can strangle an offensive far from the actual fighting.

| Supply factor | Effect on the front | Countermeasure |
|---|---|---|
| Cut supply line | Enemy units starve and stall | Guard rail and road junctions |
| Captured depot | Fuels your own advance | Send a mobile unit to seize it early |
| Terrain bottleneck | Slows the flow of supply | Pre-position stock before the assault |
| Overextended spearhead | Units run low mid-attack | Pull back and consolidate first |

The practical rule is simple: if a formation is running low, pull it back before the enemy notices. Battleplan grand scale battles reward patience and punish momentum for its own sake. A stalled tank battalion is a liability; a rested one is a threat.

## Time Control: Pause, Play, and Fast-Forward

Missions are simulated second by second, which sounds stressful until you realize you own the clock. You can pause, run in real time, or fast-forward at will. Mission lengths run from about one week to a month of in-game time.

| Mode | Best for | Watch out for |
|---|---|---|
| Pause | Drawing complex multi-stage plans | Over-planning while the situation shifts |
| Real time | Reacting to a developing breakthrough | Losing track of the wider front |
| Fast-forward | Quiet build-up and supply phases | Missing a sudden enemy push |

This is what makes the scale manageable. You are not playing a twitch game with a thousand units on screen — you are managing a schedule, a supply chain, and a chain of command all at once.

Steam Deck compatibility is currently listed as unknown, and the game requires agreement to a third-party EULA plus a broadband connection. You can find the full listing and current player reviews on the [official Battleplan page on Steam](https://store.steampowered.com/app/3743140/Battleplan/).

A few habits that pay off early:

- **Read the elevation layer first.** Place artillery and observation before you place infantry.
- **Write plans in stages.** One giant order is harder to adjust than three smaller ones.
- **Always keep a reserve.** Counterattacks need a fresh formation, not a tired one.
- **Protect your officers.** Veterancy compounds; dead officers do not.
- **Watch supply before glory.** An advance without fuel is just a long walk.
- **Use pause generously.** There is no prize for playing in real time.

At launch, the Steam store page showed a Mixed overall rating, with roughly two-thirds of the 63 reviews positive — a reminder that a deep simulation with a steep learning curve rarely pleases everyone on day one. Player experience suggests the systems click once you stop micromanaging and start delegating.

## FAQ

**What exactly are Battleplan grand scale battles?**
They are month-long, real-time WW2 engagements where you command entire divisions across battlefields built from real-world geographic data. You draw plans on the map, and your officers and companies execute them autonomously while the simulation runs second by second.

**How long does a single mission take?**
In-game mission lengths range from roughly one week to one month of simulated time. Because you can pause and fast-forward at will, a session can be as short or as long as you want.

**Is Battleplan single-player only?**
Yes. It is a single-player strategy title with Steam Achievements, Cloud saves, and Family Sharing. There is no multiplayer component described on the store page.

**Is it a good entry point for someone new to wargames?**
It is friendlier than its realism suggests, mainly because you can pause and because your subordinates handle the tactical execution. Expect a learning curve around logistics and terrain, but the drawing-based planning system is far more intuitive than micromanaging hundreds of individual units.
