A Battleplan campaign is not a skirmish you win with faster clicks. It is a weeks-long simulation of a World War II offensive, and the commanders who thrive are the ones who plan before they fight. If you have ever watched a division stall out because you forgot where its supply came from, this guide is written for you.

Everything below focuses on the Battleplan campaign specifically: how missions are structured, how to build an order of battle that survives attrition, how to draw multi-stage plans that your officers can actually execute, and how to keep logistics from quietly deciding the war for you.

## What the Battleplan Campaign Actually Simulates

A Battleplan campaign is built on a premise that separates it from most real-time strategy games: you command entire divisions, not individual squads. Missions are simulated second by second, and a single scenario can span anywhere from one week to a month of in-game time. Hundreds of thousands of troops move, fight, and run out of fuel on battlefields generated from real-world geographic data.

You do not micromanage every rifleman. Instead, you issue high-level orders and let your officers and companies carry them out autonomously. Your real job is narrower and much harder: decide where the weight of the attack goes, when it lands, and how it stays supplied — then adapt when reality disagrees with the plan.

According to the [official Steam store page for Battleplan](https://store.steampowered.com/app/3743140/Battleplan/), the game released on October 12, 2026, developed by Foolish Mortals Games Inc. and published by Slitherine Ltd. Community reports on that page describe an early "Mixed" reception among the first wave of reviewers, which is worth knowing before you commit dozens of hours to a long campaign.

**Table 1: What you control vs. what the simulation handles**

| Layer | You decide | The simulation handles |
|---|---|---|
| Strategic | Which divisions join, requisitions, upgrades | Attrition, replacements, unit fatigue |
| Operational | Objectives, phase timing, axis of advance | Movement, engagement, autonomous company actions |
| Logistical | Depot priorities, when to pull back | Supply flow across terrain, consumption rates |
| Command | Who leads each division, promotions, dismissals | Order execution, veterancy growth, officer quirks |

The takeaway: you are managing a system, not a squad. Every layer feeds the one below it, which is why a bad supply decision in week one can destroy an attack in week three.

## Building an Order of Battle That Survives a Long Campaign

Each division is modeled with a headquarters, regiments, battalions, and companies. You can combine divisions into corps for weight of effort, or split them down to companies for precision. Throughout the campaign you requisition new units, upgrade existing ones, and shuffle the chain of command as your needs change.

Because a Battleplan campaign runs long, resist the urge to build one "perfect" division. Attrition is constant, and replacements arrive into a structure you already defined. A balanced division with organic anti-tank and engineering support will outperform a tank-heavy formation that cannot cross a river or hold ground overnight.

**Table 2: Unit roles and how to use them in a long campaign**

| Unit type | Primary job | Campaign tip |
|---|---|---|
| Infantry | Hold ground, absorb pressure, clear towns | Keep at least one fresh regiment in reserve at all times |
| Tanks | Break lines, exploit gaps, counterattack | Never commit armor without infantry screening |
| Engineers | Breach obstacles, bridge, reduce fortifications | Attach at division level, not corps — you will need them locally |
| Anti-tank guns | Blunt armored counterattacks | Place on reverse slopes near likely enemy axes |
| Artillery | Preparatory bombardments, interdiction | Stockpile ammo before a set-piece attack |
| Scouts | Reconnaissance, screening | Use them to confirm terrain before you draw the plan |

Officers matter as much as hardware. You can hire and fire division commanders, and their advice is worth reading — though not always worth following. Officers who survive combat accumulate veterancy, which improves performance, and quirks, which can be a gift or a liability depending on the officer.

## Drawing Multi-Stage Plans: The Core Skill of Every Battleplan Campaign

The defining mechanic of a Battleplan campaign is that you draw orders directly on the map. Plans can be multi-step and multi-unit, which means you can script an entire operation — bombardment, assault, exploitation, consolidation — before the first shell lands.

Common tactical patterns the game supports include defense in depth, counterattacks, and planned withdrawals. The last one is underused. A withdrawal that is ordered in advance and drawn on the map is a controlled maneuver; a withdrawal you improvise under fire is a rout.

**Table 3: Plan elements and when to use them**

| Plan element | When to use it | Common mistake |
|---|---|---|
| Preparatory bombardment | Before any set-piece attack on a fortified line | Firing too short — the enemy recovers before you arrive |
| Set-piece assault | Against a known, fixed position | Committing the whole division on one axis |
| Defense in depth | After an advance outruns its supply | Defending a single line instead of layered positions |
| Planned withdrawal | When supply is failing or flanks are exposed | Waiting until units are already engaged |
| Counterattack | After an enemy attack has spent itself | Launching it before the enemy's artillery is displaced |
| Split-axis advance | When you need multiple objectives at once | Splitting so far that neither prong can hold |

**A practical sequence for drawing a plan:**

1. Recon first. Send scouts to confirm terrain, roads, and enemy positions before you commit ink to the map.
2. Set the objective. Decide what "won" looks like for this phase — a town, a crossroads, a supply depot.
3. Sequence the phases. Bombardment, assault, exploitation, consolidation. Each phase gets its own drawn orders.
4. Define the stop line. Tell units where to halt and dig in, or they will keep advancing into unsupported ground.
5. Hold a reserve. Keep at least one regiment uncommitted so you have something to answer a surprise.
6. Check the supply overlay. If the plan outruns the depots, redraw it before you execute.

## Logistics Decide the Battleplan Campaign

Supply in this game is not an abstraction bolted on top of combat. Terrain dictates how supply flows, which means a road network you ignored during planning becomes the reason your offensive stalls. You can starve an enemy by cutting their supply lines, and you can fuel your own advance by capturing their depots.

The practical rule is simple: pull back when your units run low. Pushing a depleted division forward does not produce a breakthrough — it produces a destroyed division and a gap in your line.

**Table 4: Reading supply symptoms and responding**

| Symptom | Likely cause | Response |
|---|---|---|
| Units slow to a crawl | Extended supply line, poor roads | Capture a closer depot or pause the advance |
| Artillery stops firing | Ammunition consumption outpacing flow | Rotate batteries out, prioritize the main axis |
| Repeated local failures | Supply crossing contested terrain | Secure the corridor before resuming |
| Enemy resistance collapses | You cut their supply lines | Exploit immediately — the window is short |
| Advance succeeds but cannot hold | No depot captured behind the front | Consolidate and bring supply forward first |

Mastering this loop is what turns a competent Battleplan campaign into a dominant one. Most failed offensives in this game are not beaten by the enemy — they are beaten by distance.

## Officers, Veterancy, and Time Controls

A Battleplan campaign runs in real time, but it gives you full control over the clock. You can pause, play at normal speed, or fast-forward at will. Use all three. Pause when you are drawing plans or reacting to a collapse. Fast-forward through quiet consolidation phases when nothing is at stake. Play at normal speed during the decisive hour of an assault, when a few minutes of inattention can cost you a regiment.

Officer management deserves the same deliberate attention. Veterancy is a compounding asset, so keeping experienced commanders alive is usually worth more than a slightly better position on the map.

**Table 5: Time controls and officer decisions**

| Situation | Time setting | Reasoning |
|---|---|---|
| Drawing a multi-stage plan | Paused | Orders drawn in haste produce gaps in the line |
| Quiet consolidation | Fast-forward | Nothing is being decided; save your attention |
| Set-piece assault in progress | Normal speed | Small changes matter here |
| Enemy counterattack developing | Paused, then normal | Pause to assess, then watch execution |
| Officer loses their division | Immediate review | Reassign veterans rather than promoting on seniority |

**Table 6: Officer management do's and don'ts**

| Do | Don't |
|---|---|
| Read officer advice before major operations | Follow advice blindly — quirks distort judgment |
| Keep veteran officers in command of critical divisions | Rotate proven commanders into quiet sectors |
| Dismiss officers whose quirks clash with your doctrine | Keep a liability because of one good trait |
| Track which officers gain veterancy during a campaign | Treat all commanders as interchangeable |

## Frequently Asked Questions

**How long does a single Battleplan campaign mission take?**
Missions range from roughly one week to a month of in-game time, simulated second by second. Because you can pause and fast-forward, the real-time commitment depends on how much detail you want to manage — but expect a single scenario to occupy multiple play sessions if you play it carefully.

**Can you pause during combat in a Battleplan campaign?**
Yes. The game supports pausing, real-time play, and fast-forwarding at will. Pausing is not a crutch here — it is the intended way to draw complex multi-stage orders without losing the initiative.

**Do officers really matter, or can I ignore them?**
They matter. Officers execute your drawn plans autonomously, and their veterancy and quirks shape how well they do it. Community reports from early players suggest that command-chain management is one of the deeper systems in the game, and it rewards attention over a long campaign.

**Is the Battleplan campaign multiplayer?**
The store listing currently describes a single-player experience, with Steam achievements, cloud saves, and family sharing supported. If you are looking for competitive play, treat the campaign as a solo simulation of large-scale operations rather than a head-to-head mode.

**What is the single biggest mistake new commanders make?**
Advancing past their supply. Terrain dictates how supply flows, and a division that outruns its depots will stall, then collapse. Draw your stop lines in advance, capture depots behind the front, and pull back the moment your units run low.
