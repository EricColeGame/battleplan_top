Battleplan siege missions are what happen when a WW2 objective sits behind fortifications, a river line, or a defended town — and you have to take it anyway. This historical real-time strategy game simulates a week to a month of fighting second by second, and its hardest objectives reward preparation far more than reflexes. Learning how Battleplan siege missions actually work is the difference between grinding a division into a trench line and collapsing a defense in three simulated days.

There is no menu tab labeled "siege." The term describes a *category* of campaign mission, not a separate mode, and recognizing one early changes how you build your force.

## What Counts as a Siege Mission in Battleplan

Sieges emerge from the campaign map. Any objective where the defender occupies prepared ground and you must dig them out qualifies. Because battlefields are modeled from real-world geographic data — every road, town, forest, and swamp placed where it actually exists — the ground you are attacking is genuinely the ground that was fought over.

That detail matters more than it sounds. A defended town is not a generic cluster of buildings; it is a specific place with specific approaches. Real elevation maps let you place artillery on actual high ground, and the [official Battleplan page on Steam](https://store.steampowered.com/app/3743140/Battleplan/) confirms the simulation is built at that level of fidelity.

| Situation | What makes it a siege | Your real pressure |
|---|---|---|
| Fortified defensive line | Prepared positions with interlocking fire | Breaking through without bleeding out |
| Urban strongpoint | Close terrain, few viable approaches | Time and casualties |
| River crossing or bridgehead | Chokepoints and exposed approaches | Getting across before reserves arrive |
| Supply hub assault | Defenders can replenish mid-fight | Cutting the depot before you attack |
| Encirclement | Trapped defenders who still fight hard | Closing the ring faster than relief arrives |

Mission lengths range from one week to a month, so a siege is rarely one battle. It is a sequence of decisions, and each one compounds.

## The Systems That Carry an Assault

Three systems do the heavy lifting: planning, delegation, and time control.

**Planning.** You draw multi-step, multi-unit plans directly on the map. The game supports defence in depth, counterattacks, planned withdrawals, and preparatory bombardments before a set-piece attack. You can also split a division to capture several objectives at once instead of pushing one broad front.

**Delegation.** You issue high-level orders; officers and companies carry them out autonomously. Your job is intent and timing, not micromanagement. That is why a badly drawn plan fails loudly and a good one runs itself.

**Time control.** Every mission is simulated second by second, and you can pause, run real time, or fast-forward at will.

| Control | Best used for | Siege example |
|---|---|---|
| Pause | Re-planning after contact | Redrawing the assault phase once recon reports back |
| Real time | Reacting to developments | Committing your reserve the moment a breach opens |
| Fast-forward | Quiet stretches | Waiting out artillery preparation or a supply buildup |

A common trap is treating fast-forward as a strategy. The moment a breach opens is often a two-minute window in real time.

## Building the Force for Battleplan Siege Missions

Each division is modeled with headquarters, regiments, battalions, and companies. You customize the entire order of battle and chain of command, combining divisions into corps or splitting them down to companies. Units are requisitioned and upgraded across a campaign, and officers can be hired or fired.

Officers matter beyond flavor. Keep them alive and they gain veterancy — and quirks. They also offer advice, which you are free to ignore. Community reports suggest that dismissing a veteran officer to fix one bad recommendation is usually a net loss.

| Unit | Siege job | Watch out for |
|---|---|---|
| Infantry | Holding ground, clearing strongpoints | Attrition when caught in the open |
| Tanks | Breakthrough and exploitation | Narrow streets and hidden anti-tank guns |
| Engineers | Breaching obstacles, clearing paths | Exposed while working |
| Anti-tank guns | Blunting the inevitable counterattack | Slow to reposition |
| Artillery | Preparatory bombardment, suppression | Supply-hungry and revealing |
| Scouts | Finding the weak point | Fragile under fire |
| HQ and officers | Keeping orders flowing down the chain | Losing a veteran leader mid-assault |

A balanced combined-arms division outperforms a specialist stack almost every time. Sieges are attrition problems, and attrition punishes one-dimensional forces.

## A Six-Phase Plan for Battleplan Siege Missions

Experienced commanders tend to run the same loop. The phases below work whether you are cracking a fortified line or reducing a town.

| Phase | Goal | What to draw | Failure sign |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Recon | Find the weak point | Scout orders along the whole front | Attacking blind |
| 2. Suppress | Soften known positions | Artillery missions on identified strongpoints | Infantry moving before the guns finish |
| 3. Breach | Open one gap | Engineer plus infantry plan on the narrowest sector | Attacking on a broad front |
| 4. Isolate | Cut enemy supply | Orders aimed at the depot or road junction | Defenders resupplying mid-assault |
| 5. Exploit | Push through | Split the division toward multiple objectives | Stopping at the first objective |
| 6. Consolidate | Hold the gain | Defence in depth behind the new line | Overextending into a counterattack |

The order matters. Phase 4 is the one players skip most often, and it is usually the reason a promising assault stalls three simulated days later.

## Logistics Is the Quiet Decider

Supply in Battleplan is realistically simulated, and terrain dictates how it flows. You starve an enemy by cutting their supply lines and fuel your own advance by capturing supply depots. When your units run low, the correct answer is often to pull back — not to push harder.

In a siege, the defender usually has the shorter supply line. You are the one stretching. That asymmetry is the whole problem, and it means you generally have two viable levers: out-supply them, or cut them off.

| Supply decision | When it makes sense | The trade-off |
|---|---|---|
| Push on low supply | The enemy is collapsing too | Risk of a stalled, unsupported attack |
| Pull back and refit | You have time and no urgent objective | Gives the defender time to dig deeper |
| Detach a force for a depot | The depot is close and lightly held | Weakens your main assault |
| Ignore supply entirely | You can win before it matters | Catastrophic on a month-long mission |

Elevation is part of logistics too. Artillery on high ground with a clear supply path outperforms a larger battery stuck in a swamp.

## Common Mistakes in Battleplan Siege Missions

Early Steam user reviews landed at "Mixed," and the recurring complaints from community reports tend to cluster around the same handful of habits. Most are fixable with planning discipline rather than mechanical skill.

| Mistake | Why it hurts | The fix |
|---|---|---|
| Attacking without recon | You commit to the strongest part of the line | Spend the first day on scouts |
| Overusing fast-forward | You miss the window a breach creates | Drop to real time once the guns stop |
| Treating artillery as the whole plan | Guns suppress, they do not occupy | Follow the bombardment with infantry |
| Firing experienced officers | You lose veterancy and quirks | Tolerate quirks, protect veterans |
| Ignoring elevation | Your guns are outranged and exposed | Check the real-world elevation map first |
| Stacking one sector | The enemy reinforces the obvious push | Split the division across objectives |

None of these require perfect play. They require you to slow down at the start of a mission, which is exactly what the pause button is for.

## FAQ

**Does Battleplan have a dedicated siege mode?**
No. Battleplan siege missions are not a separate game mode — they are campaign objectives where the defender holds prepared ground. The label is a useful shorthand for missions that play out as prolonged, attrition-heavy assaults rather than mobile battles.

**How long does a single siege mission take?**
Simulated mission lengths run from roughly one week to a month. Real-world playtime is far shorter because you can pause, run at normal speed, or fast-forward through quiet periods. Most of your actual time goes into planning and re-planning.

**What is the best unit for breaking a fortified line?**
No single unit does it. Engineers open the gap, artillery suppresses the defenders, infantry holds what is taken, and tanks exploit the breach. Anti-tank guns and a reserve protect you from the counterattack that always follows.

**Can you win a Battleplan siege mission without heavy artillery?**
Sometimes, but it is harder. If you cannot out-shoot the defender, out-supply them instead: cut their supply lines, take the depot, and let starvation do the work your guns cannot. Pull your own units back before they run dry, then attack again.
