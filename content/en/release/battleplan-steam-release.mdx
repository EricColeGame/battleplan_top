## What Battleplan Actually Plays Like

The core loop is unusual enough that it deserves a clear explanation. Rather than issuing orders to individual units in the traditional real-time strategy sense, you draw multi-stage plans directly onto the map. Those plans then flow down through a simulated chain of command, and your officers and companies execute them autonomously.

### Draw the plan, then let your officers execute it

Because orders are drawn rather than clicked, the game supports tactical concepts that most RTS titles cannot express well:

- **Defense in depth** — layered fallback lines drawn in advance
- **Counterattacks** — timed thrusts triggered after an enemy overextends
- **Planned withdrawals** — retreating to a prepared position rather than fleeing
- **Preparatory bombardments** — artillery softening a line before a set-piece assault
- **Multi-objective splits** — dividing a formation to take several targets at once

The result is that you operate as a commander rather than a cursor. You set intent, and the simulation handles execution — including the friction that comes when real formations try to follow real plans.

### Time control and mission length

Missions are simulated second by second, and the game lets you pause, play in real time, or fast-forward whenever you like. Mission lengths run from roughly one week to a full month of in-game time, which means a single scenario can simulate hundreds of thousands of troops across an extended operation. That scale is the headline feature of the Battleplan Steam release, and it is also the reason the system requirements stay relatively modest despite the ambition.

### Tips for your first campaign

- **Start with supply, not with the front line.** Supply is simulated realistically, and terrain dictates how it flows. Cut enemy depots before you push.
- **Use real elevation data.** The maps are built from real-world geographic data, so high ground is genuinely high ground for your artillery.
- **Protect your officers.** They gain veterancy — and quirks — if they survive, and you can hire and fire them throughout a campaign.
- **Do not ignore the historical layer.** Geo-tagged photos and videos appear where they were actually taken, which is a surprisingly effective way to learn the terrain you are fighting over.

## Army Building, Chain of Command, and Logistics

Battleplan models formations at a level of detail that will feel familiar to tabletop wargamers. Each division has a headquarters, regiments, battalions, and companies, and every element has a role: infantry, tanks, engineers, anti-tank guns, artillery, and scouts all behave differently and cooperate on their own.

| Command Level | What It Represents | Player Control |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Corps | Multiple divisions grouped for a major operation | Combine or split divisions |
| Division | The primary maneuver formation | Assign objectives, plans, officers |
| Regiment | Mid-level command within a division | Sub-objectives, task organization |
| Battalion | Tactical building block | Combat assignments |
| Company | Smallest tracked element | Splitting and detailed orders |

Between missions you requisition and upgrade units, customize your entire order of battle, and manage your officer corps. The designers have been explicit that officers give advice you are free to ignore — a small detail that adds real personality to an otherwise dry simulation.

Logistics deserves its own paragraph because it drives most outcomes. Supply lines can be severed, depots captured, and advances stalled simply because your units ran dry. Terrain decides how supply moves, so a road network you ignored during planning can become the reason a beautifully drawn offensive collapses three weeks later.
