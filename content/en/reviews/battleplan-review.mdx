Few strategy releases this year have split players as fast as Battleplan. Released on October 12, 2026, by publisher Slitherine and developer Foolish Mortals Games, it arrived on Steam with a "Mixed" rating and a very specific promise: a WW2 real-time strategy game where you win by drawing orders directly onto a map. That promise is why this Battleplan review exists — and why the verdict depends almost entirely on whether that idea clicks for you.

So here's the honest framing for any Battleplan review worth reading: this is not a game about reflexes, and it isn't trying to be. It's a game about intent. You describe what you want to happen, hand it down the chain of command, and then watch hundreds of thousands of simulated troops try to make it real. Sometimes that's exhilarating. Sometimes it's frustrating. Let's break down which is which.

## What Is Battleplan? A WW2 RTS Built Around a Pencil

Battleplan is a historical World War II real-time strategy game that simulates large, month-long battles on battlefields generated from real-world geographic data. Roads, towns, forests, and swamps sit where they actually sit. If you want to place artillery on high ground, you can study elevation maps and do exactly that.

The scale is the headline. You command entire divisions, combine them into corps, or split them all the way down to individual companies. Each division is modeled with a headquarters, regiments, battalions, and companies, and those sub-units act on their own once you issue high-level orders.

## Drawing Orders: The Feature That Makes or Breaks Battleplan

Everything in this game routes through the map. Instead of clicking a unit and right-clicking a destination, you sketch multi-step, multi-unit plans: defensive lines in depth, staged counterattacks, planned withdrawals, preparatory bombardments before a set-piece assault.

You then split divisions to grab multiple objectives at once and let your officers execute. Missions run in real time, but you can pause, play at normal speed, or fast-forward whenever you like. A single mission can span a week or a full month of in-game time.

That design creates a very particular rhythm. The planning phase is slow, deliberate, and genuinely creative. The execution phase is where things go wrong in interesting ways, because your officers are not puppets — they interpret orders, react to contact, and occasionally make choices you didn't intend.

Community reports from early buyers tend to cluster around this exact tension. Players who enjoy watching a plan unfold praise the sense of authorship. Players who want direct control describe the same system as losing agency. Both reactions are legitimate, and they explain the Mixed rating better than any single complaint.

### Time manipulation and mission pacing

| Control | What It Does | When to Use It |

| Real time | Standard simulation speed | Watching a plan develop |

Because missions are simulated second by second, fast-forward isn't a shortcut — it's a necessity. Long operational pauses are realistic, but they're also boring to watch at 1x speed.

## Army Structure, Logistics, and the Cost of Losing Momentum

The chain of command is fully customizable. You build your order of battle, requisition and upgrade units across a campaign, and hire or fire divisional officers. Officers who survive gain veterancy, which is a polite way of saying you should keep them alive — and they also pick up quirks along the way.

Combat arms work together autonomously: infantry, tanks, engineers, anti-tank guns, artillery, and scouts each handle their own tasks within the framework you set. Your job is not to micromanage a rifle squad. Your job is to give that squad a situation worth fighting in.

Logistics is where Battleplan gets genuinely nasty, in the best way. Supply is simulated with terrain dictating flow. Cut enemy supply lines and their units starve. Capture depots to fuel your own advance. Pull back when your units run dry. Push too far past your supply base and your offensive collapses on its own weight.

| System | Player Control Level | Failure Consequence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Order of battle | High — full customization | Wrong composition stalls attacks |
| Chain of command | High — hire, fire, promote | Weak officers execute poorly |
| Tactical execution | Low — officers act autonomously | Plans drift from intent |
| Logistics | Medium — via depots and terrain | Units starve, advances halt |
| Historical archive | None — purely informational | None; it's optional learning |

That last row is a small but lovely touch. Geo-tagged historical photos and videos are pinned to the real locations where they were taken, so you can learn the actual history of the ground you're fighting over.

If you're on the fence, the smartest move is to read the negative reviews specifically and ask whether the complaints describe things you'd also hate. ## FAQ

**Is Battleplan worth buying at $29.99?**
If you enjoy operational-level WW2 strategy and like the idea of drawing plans rather than micromanaging units, yes. If you want fast, direct tactical control, the price won't change how the game feels. A Battleplan review can only tell you so much — your tolerance for autonomous officers is the real deciding factor.

**Does Battleplan have multiplayer?**
No. It's a single-player game with Steam Cloud support, Family Sharing, and 53 achievements, but no competitive or co-op modes at launch.

**How long are missions in Battleplan?**
Individual missions simulate a week up to a month of in-game time, running second by second in real time with pause and fast-forward available at will.

**What are Battleplan's system requirements?**
Minimum is a 4 GB RAM, DirectX 11 machine with an Intel Celeron G4920 or AMD Athlon 200GE and a GT 1030-class GPU, plus 8 GB of storage. Recommended bumps you to 8 GB RAM and a quad-core i3-8100 or Ryzen 3 1200.

**Why is Battleplan rated Mixed on Steam?**
Community reports point to two camps: players who love the plan-and-watch loop, and players who miss direct control. At the time of writing, 66% of 63 user reviews were positive — a split that reflects design taste more than technical failure.

## The Bottom Line

Battleplan is one of the more interesting WW2 strategy experiments to land this year, and it's priced like a game that knows exactly who it's for. It asks you to think like a corps commander instead of a squad leader, and it punishes sloppy logistics harder than sloppy aiming.

The Mixed launch rating is fair, but it's also early. If the idea of sketching a multi-stage offensive across real terrain and then watching your officers either nail it or botch it sounds appealing, this is worth your $29.99. If you need your hands on every trigger, save your money — and maybe check back after a few patches.
